6.1

Dataset

  1. Tschandl, P. Rosendahl, C. & Kittler, H. (2018). The HAM10000 dataset, a large collection of multi-source dermatoscopic images of common pigmented skin lesions. Scientific Data, 5, 180161.

    doi:10.1038/sdata.2018.161 · Nature · arXiv:1803.10417

    Supports: dataset composition, 10,015 images, seven diagnostic categories, class counts, and the 58:1 imbalance ratio.

6.2

Machine learning method

  1. Kumar, A. Raghunathan, A. Jones, R. Ma, T. & Liang, P. (2022). Fine-Tuning can Distort Pretrained Features and Underperform Out-of-Distribution. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), Oral.

    arXiv:2202.10054 · OpenReview

    Supports: the LP-FT protocol; the reported result that across ten distribution-shift datasets fine-tuning averaged 2% higher in-distribution but 7% lower out-of-distribution accuracy than linear probing.

  2. Tan, M. & Le, Q. V. (2019). EfficientNet: Rethinking Model Scaling for Convolutional Neural Networks. International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML).

    arXiv:1905.11946

    Supports: the backbone architecture and its compound-scaling rationale.

  3. Ioffe, S. & Szegedy, C. (2015). Batch Normalization: Accelerating Deep Network Training by Reducing Internal Covariate Shift. ICML.

    arXiv:1502.03167

    Supports: the distinction between learnable affine parameters and running statistics that underlies the frozen-BatchNorm decision.

6.3

Machine learning in dermatology

  1. Esteva, A. Kuprel, B. Novoa, R. A. Ko, J. Swetter, S. M. Blau, H. M. & Thrun, S. (2017). Dermatologist-level classification of skin cancer with deep neural networks. Nature, 542(7639), 115–118.

    doi:10.1038/nature21056 · PMID 28117445 · Nature

    Supports: 129,450 clinical images across 2,032 diseases; comparison against 21 board-certified dermatologists.

  2. Tschandl, P. et al. (2019). Comparison of the accuracy of human readers versus machine-learning algorithms for pigmented skin lesion classification: an open, web-based, international, diagnostic study. The Lancet Oncology, 20(7), 938–947.

    MedUni Vienna summary

    Supports: 511 human readers compared against 139 algorithms from 77 laboratories, with the best algorithms outperforming human readers on the benchmark.

  3. Tschandl, P. et al. (2020). Human–computer collaboration for skin cancer recognition. Nature Medicine, 26, 1229–1234.

    doi:10.1038/s41591-020-0942-0 · Nature Medicine

    Supports: the argument that the productive configuration is clinician-supported-by-algorithm rather than algorithm alone.

6.4

Epidemiology and prognosis

  1. International Agency for Research on Cancer (2024). GLOBOCAN 2022: Melanoma of skin fact sheet. Global Cancer Observatory, World Health Organization.

    gco.iarc.who.int

    Supports: 331,722 new melanoma cases and 58,667 deaths worldwide in 2022.

  2. Salah, S. et al. (2026). Analysis of global skin cancer epidemiology in 2022 and correlation with dermatologist density. Journal of the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology.

    Wiley Online Library

    Supports: approximately 1.2 million non-melanoma skin cancer cases and 69,416 deaths globally in 2022; comparison of melanoma and NMSC mortality burden.

  3. International Agency for Research on Cancer. Global burden of cutaneous melanoma incidence attributable to ultraviolet radiation.

    iarc.who.int

    Supports: more than 80% of cutaneous melanoma attributable to UV exposure, approximately 267,000 of 332,000 cases in 2022.

  4. American Cancer Society. Survival Rates for Melanoma Skin Cancer, by Stage. Based on SEER programme data.

    cancer.org

    Supports: five-year relative survival of >99% (localized), ≈76% (regional) and ≈35% (distant). This is the central quantitative argument of the project.

  5. T.C. Sağlık Bakanlığı, Halk Sağlığı Genel Müdürlüğü. Türkiye Kanser İstatistikleri.

    hsgm.saglik.gov.tr

    Supports: Turkish melanoma incidence of 1.9 (men) and 1.3 (women) per 100,000; non-melanoma skin cancer incidence of 23.4 and 15.5 per 100,000; approximately 20,000 annual NMSC diagnoses.

6.5

Dermoscopy

  1. Vestergaard, M. E. Macaskill, P. Holt, P. E. & Menzies, S. W. (2008). Dermoscopy compared with naked eye examination for the diagnosis of primary melanoma: a meta-analysis of studies performed in a clinical setting. British Journal of Dermatology, 159(3), 669–676.

    DARE structured abstract, NCBI Bookshelf

    Supports: relative diagnostic odds ratio of 15.6 (95% CI 2.9–83.7) for dermoscopy versus naked-eye examination in melanoma diagnosis.

  2. Reiter, O. et al. (2019). The diagnostic accuracy of dermoscopy for basal cell carcinoma: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology.

    ScienceDirect

    Supports: BCC sensitivity rising from 66.9% to 85% and specificity from 97.2% to 98.2% when dermoscopy is added to naked-eye examination; pooled dermoscopy sensitivity 91.2% and specificity 95%.
