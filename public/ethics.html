Statement of status

DermAI is an undergraduate research prototype. It is not a medical device, holds no regulatory approval in any jurisdiction, has not been clinically validated, and must not be used to inform any decision about seeking or deferring medical care. It is published for academic and educational purposes.

5.1

Regulatory status

Software intended to diagnose, prevent, monitor, predict or treat disease falls within the definition of a medical device under the EU Medical Device Regulation (2017/745) and, in Türkiye, under the regulations administered by TİTCK (Türkiye İlaç ve Tıbbî Cihaz Kurumu). Diagnostic software of this type would attract a risk classification requiring conformity assessment, clinical evaluation, a quality management system, and post-market surveillance.

This project has undertaken none of those processes and makes no claim to have done so. The distinction it relies on is one of intended purpose: DermAI is presented as a research artefact demonstrating a training methodology, not as a tool intended for diagnosis. That distinction is only meaningful if the presentation is consistent with it, which is why the disclaimer appears on every page that returns a prediction, rather than once in a footer.

5.2

Distribution shift is the primary technical limitation

The training distribution is contact dermoscopy: magnified, polarised or immersion-illuminated images captured by clinicians under controlled conditions. Three categories of input fall outside it.

  • Smartphone photography. Different optics, illumination, working distance and colour processing. The subsurface structures dermoscopy reveals are simply absent. The model has no mechanism to signal that an input is unfamiliar. Softmax always normalises to one, so an out-of-distribution image yields a confident distribution rather than an abstention.
  • Other acquisition hardware. Different dermatoscopes differ in polarisation, colour rendition and magnification. HAM10000 is multi-source, which helps, but does not constitute validation against arbitrary devices.
  • Anatomical sites and lesion types under-represented in the archive. Acral, mucosal, nail-apparatus and scalp lesions have distinctive dermoscopic vocabularies that a general model trained on predominantly trunk and limb lesions will read poorly.

A concrete demonstration

Presented with uniform random noise (an input containing no lesion at all) the deployed model returns melanocytic nevus with approximately 98.6% probability. It is not detecting a mole; it is falling back on the majority-class prior in the absence of recognisable features. High reported confidence is therefore not evidence that the input was appropriate.

5.3

Representation and demographic bias

HAM10000 was assembled principally from Austrian and Australian clinical populations. Both skew heavily toward lighter Fitzpatrick phototypes. The dataset does not record phototype, so the imbalance cannot be measured directly from the metadata, which is itself part of the problem.

This has documented clinical consequence. Melanoma in darker skin presents disproportionately as acral lentiginous melanoma, on palms, soles and nail beds, with a dermoscopic appearance distinct from the superficial spreading pattern that dominates these archives. Survival outcomes are already worse in these groups, and a screening aid trained on a non-representative archive risks widening rather than narrowing that gap.

Unvalidated for the population it would serve

No performance claim on this site is stratified by skin phototype, because the data to make one does not exist in the archive used. Deployment in Türkiye (a population with a substantially different phototype distribution from the training source) would require validation on local data before any clinical claim could be entertained. This is stated as a limitation of the present work and a precondition for any continuation of it.

5.4

The asymmetry of harm

The two error modes of a screening tool produce quite different harms, and only one of them is visible to the person affected.

False negative: the serious failure

  • A malignant lesion is reported as benign.
  • The user's existing concern is displaced by an apparently authoritative reassurance.
  • Presentation is deferred, potentially across the survival gradient from localized to regional or distant disease.
  • The harm is invisible at the time it occurs, and attributable to the tool only in retrospect.

False positive: the tolerable failure

  • A benign lesion is flagged as concerning.
  • Costs an outpatient appointment, possibly a biopsy, and a period of anxiety.
  • Consumes clinical capacity, which at population scale is a genuine cost.
  • But the error is corrected by the examination it prompts.

This asymmetry is why the interface never displays an unqualified "benign" verdict. The low-risk band is worded as an absence of detected malignant indicators, accompanied by an explicit statement that this is not clearance and that a changing or symptomatic lesion warrants examination regardless of the output.

5.5

Automation bias

People systematically over-trust automated outputs, particularly when those outputs are numerically precise and delivered through a confident interface. A probability quoted to one decimal place carries an implicit claim to calibration that this model has not demonstrated.

The design responds to this in three ways: the full distribution across all seven classes is shown rather than a single verdict, so the reader sees how much probability mass sits elsewhere; the risk band is described as a deterministic aggregation with stated thresholds rather than as a clinical judgement; and the limitations are placed alongside the result rather than behind a link. None of this eliminates the effect. It is a mitigation, not a solution.

5.6

Opacity and the absence of explanation

The model provides no account of why it produced a given output. There is no saliency map, no attention visualisation, no attribution to recognisable dermoscopic criteria. A clinician cannot inspect its reasoning, and therefore cannot identify when that reasoning is spurious.

This matters more than it might appear. Dermoscopic archives are known to contain confounders (surgical skin markings, rulers and scale bars, adhesive fiducials) that correlate with malignancy because clinicians annotate lesions they already suspect. A classifier can learn the marking rather than the lesion and score well on internal validation while having learned nothing transferable. This project has not audited for such shortcuts, and their absence cannot be assumed. Gradient-based attribution would be the appropriate next step.

5.7

Data protection

Under Turkish law (KVKK, Law No. 6698), data concerning health is classified as special-category personal data, özel nitelikli kişisel veri, and is subject to stricter processing conditions than ordinary personal data. An image of a person's skin lesion, submitted in a health context, falls within that category. Equivalent protections apply under GDPR Article 9.

The deployed tool is designed so that these obligations are minimised by architecture rather than managed by policy:

  • No persistence. Uploaded images are decoded into memory, used for a single forward pass, and discarded. Nothing is written to disk.
  • No logging of content. Neither images nor predictions nor derived features are recorded.
  • No accounts, no identifiers. The tool collects no name, contact detail, or persistent identifier, so submissions are not linkable to a person.
  • No third-party transmission. Images are not sent to any external service.
  • Location stays client-side. Geolocation, if granted for the clinic finder, is read in the browser and used only to construct outbound map links. Coordinates never reach the server.
  • Client-side downscaling. Images are reduced in the browser before transmission, limiting what crosses the network in the first place.

Any future version that retained images (for instance to accumulate a local validation set) would change this analysis entirely and would require explicit informed consent, a lawful basis under KVKK Article 6, and institutional ethics approval before a single image was stored.

5.8

Dataset licence and attribution

HAM10000 is distributed under a Creative Commons non-commercial licence. Two obligations follow, and both are observed here: the dataset authors are cited wherever the data is described, and the work is not deployed commercially. Anyone building on this project should confirm the current licence terms at the source before any use beyond academic research.

Tschandl, P. Rosendahl, C. & Kittler, H. The HAM10000 dataset, a large collection of multi-source dermatoscopic images of common pigmented skin lesions. Scientific Data 5, 180161 (2018).

5.9

Conditions under which this work could progress

Stated as a research agenda rather than a disclaimer: the following would each be necessary, and none alone sufficient, before this approach could be advanced toward any clinical setting.

  1. 1

    External validation

    Evaluation on an independent archive not used in training or model selection, with a genuinely held-out test partition separate from the split used for checkpointing.

  2. 2

    Stratified performance reporting

    Metrics disaggregated by Fitzpatrick phototype, anatomical site and lesion subtype, on data where those attributes are recorded.

  3. 3

    Calibration analysis

    Reliability diagrams and calibration error, so that a stated probability corresponds to an observed frequency, a precondition for any threshold to be defensible.

  4. 4

    Out-of-distribution detection

    A mechanism for the system to decline to answer, rather than returning a confident prior when shown an input it cannot interpret.

  5. 5

    Interpretability audit

    Attribution analysis to establish that predictions rest on lesion morphology rather than acquisition artefacts or clinical markings.

  6. 6

    Prospective evaluation with clinical oversight

    Institutional ethics approval, informed consent, and a reader study measuring whether clinician performance improves with the tool rather than whether the tool performs well alone.
